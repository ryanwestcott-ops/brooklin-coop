Control the hazard, not just the outcome.

A fictional store has an extension lead across a walkway. What could happen? Compare a warning sign with removing the need for the lead or using a professionally installed safe arrangement.

Your role is to recognize and report. Do not modify wiring, guards or equipment yourself.

Time: Approx. 15 minutes.

Recognize → Assess → Control → Check

  • Recognize: what could cause harm?
  • Assess: who could be exposed, how likely is harm, and how serious could it be? Mark unknowns.
  • Control: what should be changed to prevent harm?
  • Check: how will responsible staff confirm the controls work without introducing new hazards?

Hazards may be safety-related (unguarded machinery), physical (noise or heat), chemical (cleaning products), biological (infection exposure), ergonomic (awkward lifting), or psychosocial (threats or excessive demands). Categories can overlap.

Consider controls in this order

  1. Elimination: remove the hazard.
  2. Substitution: replace it with a safer option, checking for new risks.
  3. Engineering: separate people from the hazard through design, barriers or guards.
  4. Administrative: procedures, training, scheduling and supervision.
  5. PPE: correctly selected, fitted and used protective equipment.

Controls are often combined. PPE may be required, but it is not the whole plan. Do not choose a chemical, glove or respirator by guessing.

CCOHS: hierarchy of controls

Everyone has a role

Employers provide a safe work system, information and necessary protection. Supervisors explain hazards and ensure safe procedures are followed. Workers follow training, use required protection and report hazards or defects.

Workers have rights to know about hazards, participate in health and safety, and refuse unsafe work through the applicable process. Limits apply to certain workers and situations. Report the concern immediately, remain in a safe place as the process requires, and involve your teacher. Do not simply resume unsafe work or leave without communicating.

Reference · Use during the casebook

Read the warning. Find the details.

WHMIS communicates information about hazardous products through labels, safety data sheets and education/training. Generic learning does not replace instruction about the actual products and tasks at your workplace.

Labels

A supplier label identifies the product and supplier and provides hazard/precautionary information, with required signal words and pictograms where applicable. A workplace label generally includes a product identifier, safe-handling information and a reference to the SDS. Do not use an unknown product or invent a replacement label.

Open CCOHS labels and the sample supplier label. Complete the label questions in your workbook.

Pictograms

View the official pictogram reference. Choose two and connect each warning to the need to check the full label/SDS. For example, a flame can flag a fire hazard; corrosion can flag serious skin/eye damage or corrosivity to metals. A symbol alone does not tell you the complete risk or safe procedure.

Safety data sheets

An SDS provides detailed product information. Start with the correct product identifier; do not use a similar product’s sheet.

  • Section 2: hazard identification.
  • Section 4: first-aid measures.
  • Section 6: accidental release measures.
  • Section 7: handling and storage.
  • Section 8: exposure controls and personal protection.

CCOHS: using an SDS. Finish the workbook’s WHMIS information check.

Unknown spill, exposure or missing information? Keep clear, alert the designated person and follow the emergency plan. Seek urgent help when needed. Do not improvise cleanup, rescue, mixing or treatment.

Investigate two fictional workplace scenes. Identify hazards, prioritize risk, build stronger controls, solve a WHMIS information problem and write professional orientation questions.

Open the Safety Investigation Casebook ↗

  1. Read four workplace scene cards and choose two.
  2. Identify four hazards in each scene and justify which should be addressed first.
  3. Recommend higher-level and supporting controls for responsible workplace staff to consider.
  4. Respond to an unidentified “shop mix” bottle and identify useful SDS sections.
  5. Write a stop-and-ask message and three orientation questions.
