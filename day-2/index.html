Learning goals & success criteria

Learn to research a placement or career interest, connect your strengths to work, and plan your next steps.

I can:
  • Use two reliable sources and distinguish facts from assumptions.
  • Connect three strengths to workplace tasks using real examples.
  • Identify what needs teacher or supervisor confirmation.
  • Create a practical goal, useful questions and a clear introduction.

Find Your Direction

Read the guidance here, then open each Classroom assignment below. Write assignment answers in the assigned Google Form or Doc. The ungraded Decision Lab near the end is completed on this page.

Choose the path that fits your situation

Path A

You have discussed a specific workplace with your teacher, or a tentative placement is being arranged. Research that organization. Mark what still needs confirmation.

Path B

Your placement is still being explored. Choose an occupation or sector that interests you and compare two types of workplace. You do not need a confirmed placement.

If your teacher has assigned a path, use that one. Choose your path inside Placement Preview & Success Plan in Classroom.

How to research

  1. Start with the workplace. Use its public website to learn what it does, where it is and whom it serves.
  2. Explore the occupation. Use Job Bank career planning to check duties and requirements. Use Ontario or your region where relevant.
  3. Support your claims. Record the page title, publisher, exact URL and date checked alongside the answer in your Classroom assignment. Use at least two reliable sources.
  4. Label unknowns. Write “to confirm” for duties, training, clothing or arrangements a source does not establish. Identify who could answer.
Example: fact, assumption or question?

Fictional example: the organization’s website says it repairs bicycles. That is a fact. Assuming a co-op student repairs bicycles independently is not supported. A useful question is: “Which tasks could I do, and what training would I receive?”

An occupation profile does not authorize you to perform every task listed. Your teacher confirms placement arrangements, and your workplace confirms permitted tasks and required training.

Help with strengths, goals and introductions

Strength: “I showed ___ when I ___. At this workplace, that could help me ___.”

Goal: name one skill, safe actions, evidence of improvement and someone who can help.

Introduction: explain who you are, what you want to learn, a strength with evidence, a connection to the workplace and a useful question. Do not promise a start date.

Placement Decision Lab

Practise making a placement decision using evidence. All people and workplaces below are fictional.

  1. Read each student profile and both options.
  2. Choose your next move and explain it in the box.
  3. Open the feedback, compare your reasoning and improve one part of your response.

This activity stays on the website. It is not marked, saved or sent to your teacher. You do not need to download anything or submit it in Classroom.

1. A good fit on paper

Maya enjoys organizing equipment and helping younger children. She wants to practise explaining instructions. Her agreed availability ends at 4:00 p.m., and she relies on the bus.

Option A

Community recreation centre: a supervisor describes observing activity setup and, after instruction, helping explain games. The placement time ends at 3:45 p.m. A bus stop is nearby, but Maya has not checked the timetable.

Option B

Sports equipment store: the role involves sorting stock and answering customer questions after training. The manager wants a student until 5:00 p.m. and says, “We can probably work something out.”

What would you explore next?

Compare your reasoning

The recreation centre connects to Maya’s interests and stated availability, but the bus timetable still needs checking. The store offers relevant work too, but “probably” does not confirm a workable schedule. A strong answer names the learning opportunity, the scheduling evidence and what the teacher or workplace must confirm. Neither description is placement approval.

Revisit your response. Strengthen one explanation or question if needed. There may be more than one reasonable choice.

2. A familiar interest or a new skill?

Noah likes cars and describes himself as careful with details. His goal is to become more confident asking questions and explaining information. Both workplaces could fit his agreed travel arrangements.

Option A

Auto-parts counter: the supervisor describes learning to locate parts, check product information and observe customer conversations. Noah would practise approved customer responses after instruction.

Option B

Repair workshop: the supervisor describes observing maintenance and organizing approved tools. Noah would work closely with one technician. Opportunities for customer conversation have not been discussed.

What would you explore next?

Compare your reasoning

Either setting could be worth exploring. The parts counter appears to offer more direct practice with Noah’s communication goal. The workshop connects to his interests and attention to detail, but he needs to ask how it would support communication. The strongest answer connects evidence to his goal instead of choosing solely because both workplaces involve cars.

Revisit your response. Strengthen one explanation or question if needed. There may be more than one reasonable choice.

3. An impressive promise or a clear plan?

Avery enjoys design and wants to learn how client feedback changes a project. Avery is comparing two possible placements before discussing the options with the co-op teacher.

Option A

Small design studio: the owner promises “real client projects from day one.” No supervisor, feedback routine or beginner tasks have been identified yet.

Option B

Community print shop: the supervisor describes observing how staff clarify an order, practising on fictional sample jobs and receiving feedback twice a week. Avery thinks the shop sounds less exciting.

What would you explore next?

Compare your reasoning

A clear beginner task and feedback routine provide stronger evidence of a learning opportunity than an impressive promise alone. The studio may still be suitable, but Avery needs specific answers about supervision, tasks and feedback. A strong answer explains what is known, what remains uncertain and what to ask before making a decision.

Revisit your response. Strengthen one explanation or question if needed. There may be more than one reasonable choice.
