Perseverance and mindset.

Perseverance means continuing towards something worthwhile even when it is difficult, slow or discouraging. It is not the same as never quitting anything: good perseverance includes changing your approach and asking for help.

Watch: The Power of Belief: Mindset and Success, Eduardo Briceño, TEDxManhattanBeach (about 11 minutes). Use headphones.

Find the video ↗

The video is also attached to the workbook assignment in Classroom.

Fixed mindset

“I’m just not good at this.” Abilities feel set, so mistakes feel like proof you can’t do it. It is easier to avoid challenges.

Growth mindset

“I can’t do this yet.” Abilities grow with practice, better strategies and help from others. Mistakes show you what to work on next.

Helpful thinking is not fake positivity. Telling yourself “everything is great” when it isn’t rarely helps. What helps is a thought that is both true and useful: it names the problem and points to a next step. Effort alone is not enough either. If something isn’t working, try a new strategy or ask someone.

Thought swap

Read each unhelpful thought. Decide how you would rewrite it, then open it to compare.

“My supervisor corrected me again. I’m bad at this job.”

“I’m still learning this task. I’ll ask exactly what to change and try it that way next shift.”

“Everyone else knows what they want to do after high school.”

“Lots of people are still figuring it out. Co-op is how I’m testing what fits me.”

“I missed two logs, so there’s no point catching up.”

“Two logs is fixable. I’ll do one tonight and one tomorrow.”

“I should be fine on my own. Asking for help looks weak.”

“Asking good questions is a workplace skill. Supervisors expect it from new staff.”

These examples are written in the first person so they are easy to practise. Your own swap goes in question A5.

Put it in your own words. 2–3 sentences.

School, placement, a job, sport, family, a skill or a goal. What was hard, what did you do, and what happened? Choose an example you are comfortable sharing.

Give at least one specific example of a thought and what it can lead you to do (or avoid).

Explain how a realistic, helpful thought changes what you actually do. Connect to the video if you can.

Write one unhelpful thought you have had at school, placement or work. Then rewrite it so it is true and useful, and name one next step.

Ikigai: a reason for being.

Ikigai (ee-kee-guy) is a Japanese word that roughly means “a reason for being”: the things that make life feel worthwhile and that you do willingly because they give you satisfaction and meaning.

Watch: The Japanese Formula for Happiness: Ikigai (about 7 minutes).

Find the video ↗

The popular four-circle diagram asks where four things overlap:

  • What you love: activities you lose track of time doing.
  • What you are good at: skills, strengths, what people ask you for help with.
  • What the world needs: problems you care about, people you want to help.
  • What you can be paid for: jobs, trades and services people pay for.

A useful note: the four-circle diagram is a Western adaptation that became popular online around 2014. In Japan, ikigai often means everyday things that make life worth living, like a morning routine, a hobby, family or helping a neighbour. It does not have to be your job. Both ideas are useful: the diagram for thinking about careers, and the everyday meaning for well-being.

Use point form for B1–B4. List 3–5 items in each. Think beyond school: hobbies, family roles, part-time jobs, sports, culture, volunteering and your placement.

Include at least one skill you have built or noticed at placement.

Jobs, trades, programs or services connected to your lists. Use your placement and the people you work with for ideas.

Look for overlaps. Which circles does your co-op placement connect to? What possible career, program or next step sits closest to the middle? What is missing, and how could you explore it?

Name one small, everyday thing that makes your days feel worthwhile, and explain why.

Work–life balance

Balance doesn’t mean equal hours for everything. It means work and school don’t crowd out the things that keep you well: sleep, food, movement, people and rest. Common strategies:

  • Plan ahead. Prepare the night before. Use a calendar for shifts, school and practices.
  • Protect sleep. Most teens need 8–10 hours. Screens off before bed helps.
  • Take real breaks. Eat and step away during your break. Move your body most days.
  • Set boundaries. It is okay to say no to an extra shift or commitment when you are overloaded.
  • Stay connected. Make time for people who support you.
  • Ask early. Talk to a supervisor, teacher or someone you trust before stress builds up.

My mental health toolkit.

A toolkit is the set of things you can reach for to look after your mental health, on good days and hard days. Create a Google Slides presentation of your personal toolkit.

Include

  1. Title slide with your name.
  2. Five slides on practices you already use to support your mental health. For each: what you do, when you use it and why it helps you.
  3. Three slides on practices you would like to start. For each: what it is, why you chose it, and your first small step.
  4. One “My supports” slide: the kinds of people and places you can go to for help (for example, a trusted adult, Student Services or Kids Help Phone). You do not need to include anyone’s name or number.

Use lots of visuals on every slide and an explanation in your own words on each slide. Be creative. Transitions are optional; keep them quick.

Ideas to get started: sleep, movement, time outdoors, music, creative hobbies, journalling, breathing or grounding exercises, time with friends, pets, faith or culture, helping others, limiting scrolling, a calming playlist, talking to someone, your routine change from C4.

Visuals: use Slides’ Insert → Image → Search the web, your own photos or your own drawings. Don’t include photos of other people without their permission, or private details. You will share part of your toolkit with the class. You choose which slides.
