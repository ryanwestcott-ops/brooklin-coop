Respect and equal opportunity.

Ontario’s Human Rights Code protects equal treatment in areas such as employment. Protected grounds include race, disability, creed, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity and gender expression. This is not the complete list; which protections apply depends on the setting.

Discrimination can involve a disadvantage connected to a protected ground. A rule that looks the same for everyone can still create a barrier. You do not have to decide the legal outcome before asking for help.

Accommodation means considering individual needs and addressing barriers connected to protected grounds, up to undue hardship. Discuss placement-related support privately with your teacher. The class does not need your diagnosis or personal history.

Open OHRC Human Rights 101 — video and transcript. Use Part 1 for social areas/grounds and the accommodation portion of Part 3. Spend up to ten minutes on the selected sections; you do not need the full course or a certificate.

Answer the question about equal opportunity in the Start Here section of your Classroom Response Plan. If the player is blocked, use the transcript or the explanation above.

Recognize the concern.

  • Conflict or disrespect: disagreement or rude behaviour may need support without meeting a legal definition of harassment.
  • Workplace harassment: a pattern of unwelcome comments or conduct that the person knows, or reasonably should know, is unwelcome. It need not relate to a Code ground. Do not wait for repetition before reporting a serious concern.
  • Bullying: repeated humiliation, intimidation or exclusion may be harassment. Focus on what happened, not just the label.
  • Sexual harassment: unwelcome sexual or sex/gender-related conduct, including pressure from someone who controls opportunities. You are not required to confront that person.
  • Violence: physical force, an attempt, or a threat of force that could cause physical injury. Immediate danger requires immediate help.
  • Reasonable direction: respectful instruction or correction of work is not workplace harassment by itself.

Ontario’s workplace violence and harassment guide provides further detail.

Support your well-being

Notice when you need clarification, a permitted break, workload support or a private conversation. Ask early. You can seek help without giving personal details to classmates. Support contacts may need to share information with appropriate people to respond; they cannot promise absolute secrecy.

Choose a safe response.

  1. Get safe. Move away from danger if possible. Alert staff and use emergency procedures; call 911 when emergency services are needed.
  2. Report. Use the workplace policy and tell your co-op teacher promptly. If the supervisor is involved, use the alternative workplace contact and school support.
  3. Record facts safely. Note what happened, when, where and who was present. Do not investigate, confront someone, or take confidential records to prove a point.
  4. Follow up. Ask what happens next. If the first contact is unavailable or the concern continues, contact another appropriate workplace/school lead.

Ask your teacher to clarify a reporting route. A worksheet, email or daily log is not an emergency response.

Keep workplace information private.

Use only approved accounts, devices and systems for the task. Do not photograph clients, children, records, screens or private workplace activity without the required permission. A private social-media account does not make workplace information safe to post.

Do not paste client details, workplace records or identifiable images into personal AI tools. Removing a name may not remove identifying details. If something was shared accidentally, stop further sharing and promptly tell the supervisor and teacher. Follow approved instructions; do not circulate it or quietly try to conceal the incident.

Read the Privacy Commissioner’s social-media guidance. Answer the question about how information spreads in the Start Here section of your Classroom Response Plan.

Keep these protections distinct

The Human Rights Code concerns discrimination and equal treatment. Occupational health and safety rules address workplace safety, including violence and harassment. Employment standards cover matters such as wages and hours for covered employees.

Ontario’s ESA excludes work performed by a secondary-school student under a school-board-authorized work-experience program. Do not assume every paid-job rule applies to your placement, or that this means you have no protections. Ask your teacher about your arrangement; separate paid work requires its own review.
